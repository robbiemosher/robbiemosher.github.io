Research

I am a graduate student in physics at McGill University working under the supervision of Prof. Nikolas Provatas. My research is computational and theoretical, and at the moment I am working with phase-field crystal models of crystalline materials.

I should probably emphasize the at the moment. I am still quite new to materials science. I did not spend my undergraduate degree studying condensed matter, nor did I arrive at graduate school with a long-standing plan to work on crystalline materials. My background was mostly in more general theoretical and computational physics, with my undergraduate research eventually taking me into quantum information.

Then I started talking to Nik, started reading, and found something new to become obsessed with.

Graduate Research

My introduction to phase-field crystal modelling began while I was talking with Nik about graduate research. I started reading his book with Ken Elder, then moved on to Daniel's thesis, and somewhere in there the subject went from something I was trying to understand for graduate school to something I genuinely wanted to keep thinking about.

Phase-field crystal (PFC) models describe crystalline materials using continuous density fields while retaining the periodic structure of a crystal. This allows some atomistic features of a material to survive in a description that can reach spatial and temporal scales that would be difficult to access using fully atomistic simulations.

At least, that is the neat one-paragraph description.

Once I actually started reading the theory, I found a much larger collection of questions underneath it. Where does the free-energy functional come from? What exactly do its correlation functions represent? Which pieces are inherited from classical density functional theory, which are approximations, and how do the parameters we put into the model connect back to an actual material?

Those questions are much closer to what has really caught my attention.

My current work is exploring the thermodynamic parametrization of the binary VXPFC model presented by Daniel. Very broadly, I am interested in whether the parameters appearing in the binary model can be tied more directly to thermodynamic and structural information about the material we are trying to describe.

This has led me into classical density functional theory, direct correlation functions, structure factors, phase coexistence and, increasingly, the thermodynamics of mixtures. I am still learning a great deal of the materials physics involved, so at this stage I have considerably more questions than answers.

One question I am currently exploring is what information scattering measurements from a binary material might provide about the correlation functions appearing in the model. In a non-dilute binary system, the A-A, B-B and A-B correlations all enter the problem, and they need not remain the same as the corresponding correlations in the pure materials. How useful that observation ultimately is for parametrization is something I am still working through.

I rather like being at that stage of a problem. There is a period when you know enough to begin asking better questions but not yet enough to know which of those questions are stupid. I seem to be somewhere around there.

Undergraduate Research

My undergraduate research was in a completely different corner of physics.

Under the supervision of Dr. Malcolm Butler at Saint Mary's University, my honours thesis concerned the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd (HHL) quantum algorithm for solving systems of linear equations.

HHL is usually introduced rather elegantly: encode a linear system quantum mechanically and, under suitable conditions, prepare a quantum state proportional to its solution.

As I discovered, under suitable conditions is doing quite a lot of work in that sentence.

My thesis focused on the numerical behaviour of the algorithm and the relationship between its parameters and the fidelity of the resulting quantum state. In particular, I investigated phase-estimation precision, evolution time, eigenvalue inversion, and the condition number of the input system.

I implemented numerical simulations of the algorithm in Python using PennyLane. One of the things I found most interesting was the gap between the clean mathematical description of the algorithm and what happens when you actually try to simulate it with finite resources. Phase estimation is approximate, eigenvalue inversion can be rather unforgiving, and parameter choices that look innocent on paper can produce surprisingly complicated numerical behaviour.

That project gave me my first substantial experience using computation as a way of poking at a theoretical model until I understood it better. The subject I am poking at has changed rather dramatically since then, but apparently the habit has survived.

What I'm Interested In

I am increasingly reluctant to give a definitive list of my research interests.

When I first became interested in physics, I was fascinated by relativity, quantum mechanics and cosmology. My undergraduate research ended up being in quantum computing. Now I spend a suspicious amount of time thinking about free-energy functionals and correlation functions in crystalline materials.

I did not predict that sequence particularly well, so I see little reason to pretend I know exactly what comes next.

Right now, most of my research attention is going toward statistical mechanics, phase-field crystal modelling, computational physics and learning enough materials science to understand what I am actually modelling. I still retain a strong interest in quantum mechanics, quantum information, relativity, gravitation, mathematics and theoretical physics more generally.

The common thread is probably not a particular subfield. I like mathematical models, but more specifically I like taking them apart. Where did this equation come from? Why is this term here? What did we throw away to obtain it? Is this parameter something we can measure, something we fit, or simply something convenient? And when the model works, why does it work?

Nine years after first becoming fascinated with physics, those are still basically the questions that keep sending me down rabbit holes. The equations have just become considerably more complicated.
