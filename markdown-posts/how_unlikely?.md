<h2>Introduction</h2>

<p>
    Can you recall all of the moments in your life which, looking back from
    the present, you can say changed who you are? The moments after which your
    life was never quite the same. Have you ever wondered what your life might
    have looked like had you said no to one opportunity and yes to another;
    had you quit instead of suffering through a job that drained you; had you
    gone somewhere else, met someone else, or simply made a different decision
    on an otherwise unremarkable afternoon?
</p>

<p>
    As I write this, I am in my final two weeks in Halifax. Soon I will leave
    the city in which I have spent most of my life and move to Montreal to
    begin graduate school. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the approaching end of one
    part of my life has led me to think about the moments that brought me here.
</p>

<p>
    Some are obvious only in retrospect. Others are stranger. There are moments
    when you seem to be fully aware that something in you has changed, almost
    as though the transition occurs instantaneously. You recognize that
    whatever happens next, you cannot quite return to the person you were
    before. I have come to think of these as moments of
    <em>alea iacta est</em>: <strong>the die is cast</strong>. There is
    something unnerving about experiencing one consciously. It is almost like
    watching some part of who you were disappear while another emerges in its
    place.
</p>

<p>
    But this raises a question I find difficult to leave alone:
    <strong>how improbable is the person who emerges?</strong>
</p>

<p>
    Every human life arrives at the present through an extraordinary sequence
    of events, decisions, encounters and accidents. Change any one of them and
    perhaps almost nothing changes—or perhaps the entire subsequent trajectory
    does. We look backward and see a single continuous life, yet surrounding
    that history is an immense collection of lives we imagine we might have
    lived instead.
</p>

<p>
    What, then, does it actually mean to say that things
    <em>could have been otherwise</em>? Whether those unrealized lives were
    ever physically possible depends on surprisingly deep questions about
    probability, determinism and quantum mechanics. Before wondering what the
    odds were that any of us would arrive precisely where we are, we first
    have to ask what we mean by an alternative at all.
</p>


<h2>Classical Determinism</h2>

<p>
    For most of human history, one of the basic guiding principles of science
    was the search, given an event, for a cause of that event. This, as I will
    note in subsequent sections, is an approximation—and, as it turns out, an
    extraordinarily good approximation for the macroscopic world we ordinarily
    inhabit. However, if we extend the idea of classical physical determinism
    to the entire process of a human lifetime, from the moment of conception
    to the moment of death, it rather bluntly destroys any conventional
    semblance of free will.
</p>

<p>
    The basic stipulation of physical determinism is this: given a system in
    some state, complete information about that state, and the laws governing
    its evolution, we can in principle trace the system's behaviour backward
    and predict its future states with perfect accuracy.
</p>

<p>
    Suppose we apply this idea to a human lifetime. We begin with two
    biological parents, whom we will call Alice and Bob. We suppose ourselves
    ignorant of the enormous trajectory of events that led them to conceive
    their child, Charlie. Once Charlie has been conceived, however, a strictly
    deterministic description leaves nothing fundamentally undecided.
    Charlie's development proceeds according to the laws of chemistry and
    physics. Their physical state—including the state of their brain—is simply
    part of that evolving physical system.
</p>

<p>
    This has a rather uncomfortable consequence. Charlie's first word, the
    friends they make, the subjects they enjoy in school, the person they fall
    in love with, the career they pursue, and even the apparently insignificant
    decision to turn left rather than right on some particular morning are all
    consequences of preceding physical states. Each state produces the next
    according to the governing laws. Charlie may experience themselves as
    considering alternatives, but the process of consideration is itself
    physical: neurons fire, neurotransmitters are released, sensory information
    is processed, memories are recalled, and eventually Charlie says either
    yes or no.
</p>

<p>
    Under strict determinism, there was never a physical moment at which both
    answers could actually occur.
</p>

<p>
    This makes our moments of <em>alea iacta est</em> rather peculiar. Imagine
    Charlie receives an offer which will require them to leave their hometown.
    They agonize over it for a week before finally saying yes. Years later,
    Charlie can identify this as one of the critical moments of their life.
    Had they said no, they imagine, they would have lived somewhere else, met
    different people, and perhaps become a substantially different person.
</p>

<p>
    The counterfactual is meaningful to Charlie because they did not know what
    they were going to choose. But ignorance is not the same thing as
    indeterminacy. In a perfectly deterministic universe, the statement
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    \text{Charlie could have said no}
    $$
</div>

<p>
    cannot mean that, with the universe in precisely the same physical state,
    nature might genuinely have produced the other answer. It can only mean
    something conditional:
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    \text{Had the preceding state been different, Charlie would have said no.}
    $$
</div>

<p>
    Change Charlie's memories, circumstances, neural state, the words used in
    the offer, or some sufficiently distant link in the causal history leading
    to that moment, and a different future may follow. But hold the complete
    physical state fixed and classical determinism permits only one
    continuation.
</p>

<p>
    We can extend this argument backward. The state of Charlie at conception
    depended upon the states of Alice and Bob. Those states depended upon their
    own histories, which depended upon still earlier states of the world.
    Continue far enough and Charlie's apparently personal trajectory becomes
    embedded in the trajectory of everything that preceded them.
</p>

<p>
    This is essentially the universe imagined by Laplace's famous intellect:
    an intelligence which, knowing at one instant all the forces of nature and
    the positions of everything composing it, could encompass past and future
    within the same calculation. To such an intelligence, Charlie's life would
    contain no surprises. The uncertainty belongs to Charlie, not to the
    underlying mechanics.
</p>

<p>
    There is something almost paradoxical about looking at a life this way.
    From inside it, the future appears open. We deliberate because we do not
    know what we will do. Looking backward, meanwhile, we identify forks in
    the road and imagine the lives extending from paths we did not take. Yet
    under strict physical determinism the road never actually forked. There was
    only one trajectory through state space, accompanied by an organism
    complicated enough to imagine the neighbouring trajectories.
</p>

<p>
    In that picture, asking for the probability of Charlie arriving at some
    particular state of their life requires care. Conditional on the exact
    initial state of the universe and deterministic laws, the probability is
    not fantastically small. It is, in the relevant sense,
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    P(\text{Charlie's present state}\mid\text{complete initial state})=1.
    $$
</div>

<p>
    The apparent improbability enters only because we do not possess that
    complete information. We instead construct an ensemble of conceivable
    initial conditions, decisions and events, and discover that only a tiny
    subset seem to lead to the particular life Charlie eventually occupies.
    The uncertainty is epistemic: it describes our ignorance of the trajectory
    rather than uncertainty in the trajectory itself.
</p>

<p>
    And yet our best description of nature does not appear to grant us such a
    simple universe. When we descend far enough into the microscopic world,
    the classical picture begins to fail. Quantum mechanics forces us to
    reconsider what we mean by a possible future—and, depending on how we
    interpret the theory, whether the paths Charlie did not take were ever
    truly impossible at all.
</p>


<h2>Quantum Theory</h2>

<p>
    The theory of quantum mechanics is a beautiful and relatively recent—only
    about one hundred years old—revolution in our description of microscopic
    phenomena. Instead of assigning every microscopic particle a definite
    classical trajectory through space, quantum mechanics describes a system
    using a complex-valued object called the wavefunction,
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    \psi(\mathbf{r},t).
    $$
</div>

<p>
    The evolution of this wavefunction is governed by the time-dependent
    Schrödinger equation,
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi(\mathbf{r},t)
    +V(\mathbf{r},t)\psi(\mathbf{r},t)
    =
    i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi(\mathbf{r},t)}{\partial t}.
    $$
</div>

<p>
    For our purposes, the important feature of this equation is not its
    precise mathematical form, but something rather peculiar about what it
    predicts. The wavefunction does not generally tell us that a particle
    <em>is here</em> and <em>has this momentum</em> in the same way that
    classical mechanics assigns a particle a point in phase space. Instead,
    it allows us to calculate probabilities for the possible outcomes of
    measurements.
</p>

<p>
    For example, the probability of finding a particle within some region
    $R$ at time $t$ is
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    P(\mathbf{r}\in R\mid t)
    =
    \int_R |\psi(\mathbf{r},t)|^2\,d^3r.
    $$
</div>

<p>
    More generally, if we wish to measure some physical observable $M$,
    represented in quantum mechanics by an operator $\hat{M}$, its expectation
    value is
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    \langle M\rangle
    =
    \langle\psi|\hat{M}|\psi\rangle.
    $$
</div>

<p>
    In the position representation this may be written
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    \langle M\rangle
    =
    \int
    \psi^*(\mathbf{r},t)
    \hat{M}
    \psi(\mathbf{r},t)
    \,d^3r.
    $$
</div>

<p>
    This distinction matters. The expectation value is not generally the
    probability that the system "has the property $M$." Rather, it is the
    average value we would expect from many identically prepared measurements.
    The probabilities of particular outcomes are supplied by the Born rule:
    roughly speaking, the squared amplitude associated with an outcome
    determines the probability with which that outcome will be observed.
</p>

<p>
    At first glance this might seem like nothing more than another form of
    ignorance. Perhaps the particle really does possess some definite state,
    and the wavefunction merely describes our inability to know it. If that
    were the case, quantum probability would not fundamentally threaten the
    deterministic picture of Charlie's life from the previous section. We
    would simply have replaced our ignorance of Charlie's microscopic initial
    conditions with a more sophisticated mathematical description of that
    ignorance.
</p>

<p>
    Nature, however, appears to be considerably stranger than this.
</p>

<p>
    Quantum mechanics does not behave like an ordinary classical probability
    distribution over pre-existing values. Bell's theorem, and the experiments
    inspired by it, rule out a broad and particularly natural class of
    explanations in which measurement outcomes are determined by local hidden
    variables carried by the system all along. Whatever quantum probability
    means, it cannot simply be replaced by the familiar picture of particles
    secretly possessing all their classical properties while we remain
    ignorant of them.
</p>

<p>
    This brings us back to Charlie.
</p>

<p>
    Charlie is not, of course, a single quantum particle. A human brain is an
    enormous, warm, strongly interacting system containing on the order of
    $10^{27}$ atoms. Interactions with the surrounding environment cause
    coherence between macroscopically distinct alternatives to become
    effectively inaccessible extraordinarily rapidly through a process known
    as <em>decoherence</em>. This is one reason the world of tables, people,
    planets and bartenders appears overwhelmingly classical despite being
    constructed entirely from quantum-mechanical constituents.
</p>

<p>
    It is worth emphasizing, however, that the distinction between the quantum
    and classical worlds is not simply a distinction between things which are
    small and things which are large. Quantum mechanics does not suddenly
    cease to operate once enough atoms have been assembled.
</p>

<p>
    We have, in fact, produced fluids whose bulk behaviour is unmistakably
    quantum mechanical. Liquid helium, when cooled below a critical
    temperature, can enter a <em>superfluid</em> state in which an enormous
    number of atoms participate in collective quantum behaviour. Superfluid
    helium can flow without ordinary viscosity and exhibits phenomena that
    cannot be understood using classical fluid mechanics alone. We have
    similarly produced Bose–Einstein condensates, in which large collections
    of atoms occupy a common quantum state and exhibit coherent wave-like
    behaviour on scales large enough to observe experimentally.
</p>

<p>
    Perhaps an even stranger demonstration is that we have learned to turn
    quantum behaviour into a computational resource. A classical computer
    stores information in bits which occupy one of two logical states, $0$ or
    $1$. A quantum computer instead manipulates <em>qubits</em>, which can
    exist in coherent superpositions of computational basis states,
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    |\psi\rangle
    =
    \alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle,
    $$
</div>

<p>
    where
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1.
    $$
</div>

<p>
    By carefully controlling superposition, interference and entanglement,
    quantum algorithms can manipulate information in ways unavailable to
    ordinary classical computation. The engineering difficulty is precisely
    the phenomenon discussed above: the quantum state must remain sufficiently
    isolated from its environment to preserve the coherence on which the
    computation depends. Uncontrolled interactions produce decoherence and
    destroy the very quantum behaviour we are attempting to exploit. Quantum
    computers are therefore rather remarkable objects: deliberately
    constructed physical systems in which we fight the natural emergence of
    classical behaviour long enough to make quantum mechanics perform useful
    work.
</p>

<p>
    The classical appearance of everyday life therefore cannot simply be
    attributed to size. It emerges because macroscopic objects ordinarily
    interact incessantly with enormous environments, rapidly suppressing
    observable interference between macroscopically distinct alternatives.
    Charlie is an almost spectacular example of such an open system: warm,
    chemically active, constantly interacting with photons, air molecules,
    surrounding matter and countless internal degrees of freedom. The
    remarkable thing is not that quantum mechanics ceases to apply to
    Charlie—it never does—but that the collective behaviour of Charlie's
    roughly $10^{27}$ constituent atoms can ordinarily be described so
    extraordinarily well without keeping track of their complete quantum
    state.
</p>

<p>
    But classical appearance is not necessarily the same thing as fundamental
    classical determinism.
</p>

<p>
    Suppose some microscopic quantum event occurs somewhere in the causal
    chain leading to one of Charlie's critical moments. Perhaps the microscopic
    difference is initially absurdly small. In a sufficiently complicated
    nonlinear system, however, microscopic differences can sometimes become
    amplified into macroscopic ones. A molecular event influences the firing
    of a neuron; the altered neural activity changes the timing of some action;
    that action changes an interaction with another person; and the
    consequences continue outward.
</p>

<p>
    We should be cautious here. It would be unjustified to claim that every
    human decision is determined by some conveniently placed quantum coin
    toss. Human cognition is overwhelmingly governed by complicated biological
    dynamics, and quantum mechanics does not magically rescue the traditional
    concept of free will. Replacing
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    \text{my decision was determined}
    $$
</div>

<p>
    with
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    \text{my decision contained some physical randomness}
    $$
</div>

<p>
    does not obviously make the decision any more <em>mine</em>.
</p>

<p>
    Nevertheless, something important has changed.
</p>

<p>
    Under the strict classical picture considered previously, if we restore
    the universe to precisely the same physical state and allow it to evolve
    again under the same laws, Charlie must make precisely the same decision.
    There is only one physically permitted continuation.
</p>

<p>
    Under an interpretation of quantum mechanics involving genuinely
    stochastic measurement outcomes, that need no longer be true. The same
    quantum state may assign nonzero probabilities to several possible
    outcomes,
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    P(M_i)=|\langle M_i|\psi\rangle|^2,
    $$
</div>

<p>
    with
</p>

<div class="equation">
    $$
    \sum_i P(M_i)=1.
    $$
</div>

<p>
    If those microscopic alternatives become amplified into macroscopically
    different histories, then the future is no longer merely unknown. More
    than one future is genuinely permitted by the physical description.
</p>

<p>
    Our imagined critical juncture can therefore acquire a meaning it did not
    possess in the deterministic universe. Before Charlie says yes, there may
    really be physical histories compatible with the state of the world in
    which events unfold differently. Afterward, Charlie occupies one
    particular history and looks backward at alternatives which were not
    merely products of ignorance.
</p>

<p>
    <em>Alea iacta est.</em>
</p>

<p>
    The die has been cast.
</p>

<p>
    But here the metaphor becomes unusually literal. Quantum mechanics
    provides the weights associated with the faces of the die. What it does
    <em>not</em> settle by itself is what we should believe happens to all the
    faces that Charlie does not observe.
</p>

<p>
    One interpretation says that an outcome occurs probabilistically and the
    alternatives are no longer realized. Another takes the deterministic
    evolution of the wavefunction more seriously and reaches a considerably
    stranger conclusion: perhaps the die never selects only one face at all.
</p>

<p>
    To understand that possibility, we have to turn to the many-worlds
    interpretation.
</p>
