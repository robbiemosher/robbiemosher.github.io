#On The Scale of Life

It is a fundamental law of nature, the second law of thermodynamics, that the entropy of an isolated system does not decrease.[1] On sufficiently long scales this has rather bleak implications for the universe and, more immediately, for us: we are thermodynamic systems. We eat food, consuming chemical free energy, and perform useful work. Your body pumps blood to your brain and other extremities, fires electrical signals through neurons, repairs itself and moves matter around, all the while producing by-products including CO<sub>2</sub>, electromagnetic radiation and sound waves. In fewer words: the human body consumes <em>free energy</em> to maintain itself far from equilibrium.

This fact is not unique to the human organism; it is also applicable to what I will call the Human organism. Throughout this article I will use the lowercase <em>human</em> to mean the individual biological system, and the capitalized <em>Human</em> to mean humanity considered collectively: billions of biological systems which have learned to accumulate energy, technology and, most importantly, information across generations.

The Human organism consumes chemical free energy stored in biological matter, extracts energy from areas of rapidly moving atmosphere, harnesses energy sent to us from the Sun, and releases nuclear energy from readily fissionable material. We turn some of this energy into electrical and mechanical work; in fact I am using some of that energy right now to write this article, and you are using some more to read it.

All the while we are working, innovating and producing knowledge, we are producing entropy. Every useful thing humanity accomplishes is purchased, in some sense, through entropy production. The exact cosmological fate of the universe is more complicated than imagining a hot object simply cooling toward equilibrium, particularly in an expanding universe dominated by gravity. Nevertheless, if the universe continues toward the state usually called the <em>Big Freeze</em> or heat death, then there is something wonderfully strange about our position within that story: for a brief period, little islands of matter very far from equilibrium have appeared and begun trying to understand the system in which they exist.

We have an enormous impact on the Earth's overarching ecosystem. We span the Earth, redirect rivers, alter its atmosphere, split atomic nuclei and launch pieces of the planet into space. Yet outside the approximately closed system of the Earth, our influence is, to put it kindly, meager.

For the purpose of this writing we will define a human lifetime to be one hundred years, with $\mathrm{hl}$ being the respective unit. Taking a year to be approximately $365.25$ days,[2]

$$
1\mathrm{hl} = 100\mathrm{yr} = 1,200\mathrm{mth} \approx 5,218\mathrm{wk} = 36,525 \mathrm{d} = 876,600 \mathrm{hr} = 52,596,000 \mathrm{min} = 3,155,760,000\mathrm{s}.
$$

This is the scale from which we will look outward.

## Time

The human lifetime seems to be quite long; $3\times 10^{9}$ seconds is a large number to most any person. Compared to the time elapsed since the early universe, approximately $U=4.35\times10^{17}\mathrm{s}$, however, a person will live to see only a tiny fraction of cosmic history. The universe is approximately 13.8 billion years old.[3]

$$
\frac{1\mathrm{hl}}{U}\approx7.3\times10^{-9}.
$$

If the entire age of the universe were compressed into a single second, one human lifetime would occupy only about seven nanoseconds.

Despite this, the scale of time accessible to us extends enormously beyond the scale upon which we live. Aided by instruments such as the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), we can observe galaxies whose light has travelled for more than 13 billion years to reach us.[4] We see things happening in the past all around us. The Sun appears to us as it was approximately eight minutes ago,[2] the Moon about 1.3 seconds ago,[4] and even a person standing a few metres away is seen nanoseconds in the past.

In fact, everything you see occurred in the past.

That sentence sounds more profound than the underlying physics really is: light simply takes time to travel. But I find it remarkable nonetheless. Our experience of all things occurs in this fleeting moment while all of the information from which that experience is constructed necessarily comes from moments which have already happened.

Our reach extends in the opposite direction as well. Complex visual processing in the human brain occurs on characteristic timescales of roughly tenths of a second; experiments have demonstrated complex visual categorization within about $150\mathrm{ms}$.[5] Yet through instruments of our own construction we can resolve dynamics occurring on femtosecond and even attosecond timescales. The 2023 Nobel Prize in Physics recognized Pierre Agostini, Ferenc Krausz and Anne L'Huillier for experimental methods capable of generating attosecond pulses of light for the study of electron dynamics in matter.[6]

An attosecond is

$$
1\mathrm{as}=10^{-18}\mathrm{s}.
$$

For some perspective, there are approximately as many attoseconds in one second as there have been seconds since the beginning of the universe.

Of course, we do not <em>experience</em> an attosecond. There is no human perception quick enough to watch an electron move and think, "there it goes." Instead, we have done something much stranger: we constructed instruments which translate events occurring on scales completely inaccessible to our biology into information which a human mind can understand.

There is therefore an interesting asymmetry between the temporal scale upon which we exist and the temporal scales we are capable of studying. A human organism is confined to a lifetime of approximately $10^{9}\mathrm{s}$. The Human organism is not so restricted. Through observation, experiment and inference, we have extended ourselves across almost the entire known history of the universe in one direction and into the dynamics of electrons and atoms in the other.

The physical duration of a human life is small. The span of time accessible to the Human mind is vast.

## Space

Due to our scale we experience what appears to be only a tiny fraction of space: say your neighborhood or city, possibly a few different countries over the course of your life. From this perspective the world can appear massive. However, we need only zoom out slightly before our apparent stationary existence begins to disappear.

Consider a person standing still on the surface of the Earth. The Earth completes one rotation approximately every 23.9 hours,[2] corresponding to an angular velocity of approximately

$$
\omega\approx4.18\times10^{-3}\mathrm{\ deg/s}.
$$

At a point on the surface of the Earth at latitude $\phi$ and altitude above sea level $A$, the distance travelled due to this rotation is given by

$$
\Delta s=(R_E+A)\Delta\theta\cos(\phi),
$$

where $\Delta\theta$ is the angular distance through which the Earth has rotated and $R_E$ is the radius of the Earth. The Earth's equatorial radius is approximately $6,371\mathrm{km}$.[7] For simplicity, consider a person standing at sea level on the equator, such that $A=0$ and $\cos(\phi)=1$. Over a human lifetime this person would travel approximately $2.5\times10^8\mathrm{km}$ simply as a consequence of the Earth's rotation.

But the surface of the Earth is itself moving. Gravity continuously accelerates the Earth around the Sun at an average orbital speed of approximately

$$
v_E=29.78\mathrm{\ km/s}.
$$

NASA gives the Earth's mean orbital velocity as $29.78\mathrm{\ km/s}$.[8] A straightforward calculation therefore gives

$$
\Delta s_E=v_E(1\mathrm{hl})\approx9.40\times10^{10}\mathrm{km}.
$$

Thus, during a single human lifetime, a person who considers themselves to have spent most of their life relatively stationary is carried through approximately $10^{11}\mathrm{km}$ of orbital path around the Sun.

The Sun, however, is not stationary either. The Solar System orbits the centre of the Milky Way at roughly $8\times10^5\mathrm{\ km/h}$, completing an orbit in approximately 230 million years.[9] Taking a characteristic speed of approximately $230\mathrm{\ km/s}$, over one human lifetime we obtain

$$
\Delta s_G\approx(230\mathrm{\ km/s})(3.16\times10^9\mathrm{s})
\approx7.3\times10^{11}\mathrm{km},
$$

or approximately

$$
\Delta s_G\approx0.077\mathrm{\ ly}.
$$

So you can spend your entire life sitting "still" and nevertheless be carried a respectable fraction of a light-year around the galaxy.

There is, however, a problem hidden within all of these calculations: distance travelled through space is not absolute. We must always ask, <em>relative to what?</em>

Relative to the surface of the Earth, our stationary person travels no distance at all. Relative to the Earth's axis of rotation, they travel hundreds of millions of kilometres. Relative to the Sun, they travel approximately $10^{11}\mathrm{km}$. Relative to the centre of the Milky Way, the path is of order $10^{12}\mathrm{km}$. Other reference frames give still other answers.

There is no universal coordinate grid painted across the cosmos against which we can ask how far a human being has traveled.

Our apparent stillness is therefore a consequence of the reference frame from which we experience the world.

Yet, as with time, the spatial scale upon which the human organism exists is very different from the spatial scales accessible to the Human organism. A human being occupies a characteristic length scale of approximately

$$
L_H\sim10^0\mathrm{m}.
$$

At one extreme, modern particle physics investigates interactions occurring around and below scales of approximately $10^{-18}\mathrm{m}$; for example, the weak interaction has a characteristic range of approximately $10^{-18}\mathrm{m}$.[10] At the other, astronomical observations allow us to study an observable universe with a present-day radius of approximately 46.5 billion light-years, or of order

$$
R_U\sim10^{26}\mathrm{m}.
$$

[11]

The Human organism therefore has access, through observation, experiment and inference, to roughly forty-four orders of magnitude in spatial scale,

$$
10^{-18}\mathrm{m}\lesssim L\lesssim10^{26}\mathrm{m}.
$$

We cannot directly experience either extreme. I cannot see the internal structure of an atomic nucleus with my eyes, nor can I step far enough away from the Milky Way to look back at the whole thing. Instead, just as we constructed instruments capable of translating attosecond dynamics into human timescales, we construct instruments which translate otherwise inaccessible spatial scales into information our minds can understand.

A human being is approximately one metre in scale and lives upon a planet which seems enormous from its surface. Yet that same metre-sized organism has learned to examine nature across more than forty orders of magnitude in length.

The physical extent of a human is small. The span of space accessible to the Human mind is vast.

## Gravity

Gravity presents us with another peculiar question of scale because, unlike most of the quantities discussed so far, it is something we experience continuously. At the surface of the Earth we live within an approximately constant gravitational acceleration,

$$
g_E\approx9.81\mathrm{\ m/s^2}.
$$

[7]

This value is so thoroughly incorporated into our biology and intuition that it hardly feels remarkable. It determines how we walk, how objects fall, how our circulatory systems operate and, indirectly, much of the physical architecture of life on Earth. To a human being, Earth's gravity is simply what gravity <em>is</em>.

Yet the gravitational environments permitted by nature span an enormous range. On the Moon the surface gravitational acceleration is approximately

$$
g_M\approx1.62\mathrm{\ m/s^2}\approx0.17g_E,
$$

[7]

while on the surface of the Sun it is approximately

$$
g_\odot\approx274\mathrm{\ m/s^2}\approx28g_E.
$$

[12]

More compact objects carry us far beyond anything a human body could directly experience. At the surface of a neutron star, gravity can be roughly $10^{11}$ to $10^{13}$ times stronger than Earth's surface gravity, depending on the mass and radius of the star.[13]

At such scales our familiar description of gravity as a force pulling objects downward becomes increasingly inadequate. General relativity instead describes gravity through the geometry of spacetime itself. Matter and energy distort that geometry, while freely falling objects follow paths determined by it.[14]

Black holes take this to an extreme. Curvature can become so severe that a region is enclosed by an event horizon, beyond which no signal can return to the outside universe. Near sufficiently small black holes, differences in gravity across the length of a human body would themselves become catastrophic. What matters is no longer merely the local value of gravitational acceleration, but how rapidly the gravitational field changes from one location to another: the tidal field.[14]

Standing here on Earth, those differences are tiny. For a person of height $h$, the fractional change in Earth's gravitational field can be estimated as

$$
\frac{\Delta g}{g}\sim\frac{2h}{R_E},
$$

which for $h\sim2\mathrm{m}$ is of order $10^{-6}$. As far as our bodies are concerned, the gravitational field around us might as well be uniform.

And yet we can detect distortions of spacetime that are incomprehensibly smaller.

Modern gravitational-wave interferometers measure strain,

$$
h_{\mathrm{GW}}=\frac{\Delta L}{L},
$$

at scales around $10^{-21}$ for astrophysical gravitational waves.[15] Advanced LIGO's four-kilometre interferometers are capable of detecting changes in arm length smaller than one ten-thousandth the diameter of a proton.[16]

Again we find the same pattern. Our bodies evolved to understand a narrow gravitational environment centred around $1g$. Our mathematics and instruments allow us to study everything from the weak gravitational attraction of laboratory masses to neutron stars, black holes, gravitational waves and the geometry of the universe itself.

We live within one extraordinarily ordinary gravitational environment. Yet from that small patch of curved spacetime, the Human mind has learned to describe gravity on the scale of the cosmos.

## Information

Perhaps the strangest scale available to us is not one of distance, time or gravity, but of information.

At its simplest, information can be reduced to a distinction between alternatives: a bit, capable of occupying one of two states. From enormous collections of these elementary distinctions we construct language, mathematics, images, scientific theories, cat videos, bank accounts, this website and much of modern civilization.

The human organism itself is an information-processing system. Our sensory organs continuously receive information about the environment, while the nervous system filters, transmits and processes these signals. Whatever eventually reaches conscious thought is not the physical world itself but a remarkably compressed representation of it.

Yet the Human organism is no longer restricted by the memory or information-processing capacity of an individual brain.

Writing changed something fundamental. Information could survive the person who knew it.

Printing allowed that information to be reproduced. Telecommunications allowed it to travel around the Earth at nearly the speed of light. Computers allow quantities of information impossible for any individual person to consciously process to be stored, transmitted and manipulated. We have, in a very literal sense, constructed memory outside ourselves.

There is also a physical cost to all of this. Information is not entirely abstract; it must ultimately be represented by physical states. A bit may be encoded by electrical charge, magnetic orientation, photons, molecular configurations or any number of other physical systems. Consequently, information processing is constrained by thermodynamics.

Landauer's principle establishes that the irreversible erasure of one bit of information carries a minimum thermodynamic cost associated with

$$
E_{\mathrm{bit}}=k_BT\ln2,
$$

where $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is the temperature of the environment.[17,18]

And suddenly we are back where we started.

Entropy.

For a system described by a set of probabilities $p_i$, the Gibbs entropy can be written

$$
S=-k_B\sum_i p_i\ln p_i.
$$

In information theory, the Shannon entropy has the closely related mathematical form

$$
H=-\sum_i p_i\ln p_i,
$$

when information is measured using natural logarithms. The relationship between thermodynamic entropy and information entropy has subtleties which could consume an article of its own, but the similarity is not accidental: information must ultimately be represented and manipulated by physical systems governed by thermodynamics.[17]

This brings us back to the human.

An average human body contains on the order of $10^{28}$ atoms; a commonly quoted estimate is approximately $7\times10^{27}$.[19] But simply saying that tells us almost nothing about a person. What distinguishes you from an arbitrary pile containing roughly the same atoms is their organization: molecules, cells, tissues, neural connections and an immense history of interactions with everything around you.

And then there is everything that does not fit neatly inside the boundary of your skin: your memories, things you have written, ideas you have given other people, photographs, relationships, measurements, computer files and changes you have made to the physical world.

A human life is an extraordinarily particular state of matter.

The same is true of humanity, except the Human organism has discovered a trick unavailable to the individual human: its knowledge does not necessarily die when one of its constituent organisms does.

An individual physicist may live for only $10^9$ seconds, yet they can begin their career already knowing about processes occurring $10^{17}$ seconds in the past and events lasting $10^{-18}$ seconds. They do not need to rediscover calculus. They do not need to measure the speed of light for themselves. They do not need to derive general relativity from scratch.

Someone else did that.

Then they died.

The information remained.

This, more than anything else discussed here, may be the defining distinction between the human organism and the Human organism. Individually, we are finite thermodynamic systems occupying a remarkably narrow range of space and time. Collectively, we have developed mechanisms for accumulating information across generations. Every new human does not begin at zero.

In this sense, the Human organism can reach scales which no human organism could ever directly experience.

We live for approximately one $\mathrm{hl}$. We occupy approximately one metre of space. We experience a narrow range of gravitational fields, energies and timescales. Yet through information, a physical system of remarkably limited scale has constructed a representation of a universe extending across tens of orders of magnitude.

We began with the second law of thermodynamics. Eventually every human organism exhausts its ability to maintain itself far from equilibrium. One $\mathrm{hl}$ ends, usually quite a bit before the full hundred years allotted to it here.

But some of the information can escape.

Perhaps our significance cannot be found in the scale upon which we exist, but in the scale upon which we have learned to know.
