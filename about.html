About Me

I'm Robbie Mosher, a graduate student in physics from Nova Scotia. I completed my B.Sc. (Honours) in Physics at Saint Mary's University in Halifax and am now beginning graduate studies in physics at McGill University in Montreal.

My interests are mostly in computational and theoretical physics. My current research involves phase-field crystal modelling, and in particular I am exploring the thermodynamic parametrization of the binary VXPFC model presented by Daniel. The broader question is how the parameters of the model can be connected to the thermodynamic and structural properties of actual material systems, rather than existing simply as abstract quantities in a free-energy functional. It is still very much a problem I am learning my way into, which is part of what makes it interesting.

My undergraduate research was rather different. My honours thesis concerned the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd quantum algorithm for solving systems of linear equations. Somewhere between quantum computing and crystalline materials, I seem to have developed a fairly general affection for problems where physics, mathematics, and computation meet.

I think the common thread is that I like knowing where things come from. It is one thing to be given an equation and learn how to use it; it is another thing entirely to understand why that equation has the form it does, what assumptions went into it, and what happens when those assumptions stop being true. Most of the things I find interesting seem to reduce to some version of that question.

Outside Physics

Physics occupies a perhaps unreasonable amount of my attention, but not all of it.

I'm interested in mathematics, philosophy, computing, history, and whatever other subject has managed to send me down a rabbit hole that week. I have a particular interest in how we reason about the world and how much confidence we should actually place in the pictures we construct of it. Physics is rather good at periodically reminding us that the intuitive picture was wrong.

I also find myself thinking a lot about scale. The universe looks very different depending on whether you are talking about atoms, people, planets, or cosmological distances, and ideas that seem completely obvious at one scale can become rather strange at another. Somewhere in there you inevitably end up thinking about probability, determinism, entropy, consciousness, and what exactly we mean when we say that we understand something.

Outside of all that, I enjoy programming and tinkering with computers, reading, walking, coffee, video games, photography, and occasionally writing the sorts of thoughts that eventually end up on this website. I use Linux voluntarily, which probably says something about my relationship with computers.

A Little History

My love for physics began in high school, during one particular class on the kinematic equations of motion. My teacher at the time, Mr. Power, introduced the equations more or less as given.

For whatever reason, this bothered me.

I understood how to use them, but I wanted to know why objects near the surface of the Earth actually moved according to them. That question sent me down a rabbit hole that, in retrospect, I never really came back out of.

I discovered calculus, then Newton's law of gravitation, the principle of stationary action, general relativity, and eventually quantum mechanics. There were plenty of detours along the way, and I certainly didn't understand most of what I was reading the first time around, but by that point it didn't really matter. I was hooked.

Around the same time, I would listen to talks by Dr. Robert Thacker, an astrophysicist here in Nova Scotia. It never occurred to sixteen-year-old me that years later Dr. Thacker would be a reader for my undergraduate thesis, much less that he would eventually write me a letter of recommendation for graduate school. I certainly didn't imagine that I would complete that thesis under the supervision of Dr. Malcolm Butler, who became an incredible mentor to me and had a great deal to do with the physicist I eventually became.

The route between those two points was not especially direct.

I grew up in small-town Nova Scotia and eventually left for Carleton University. Unfortunately, much of my time there coincided with the pandemic, so my introduction to university physics involved considerably more staring at a computer screen than I had imagined. Still, it was during that period that physics started becoming something more serious for me.

It was also around then, thanks in part to an old friend, Will Kyte, that I started appreciating a more mathematically rigorous approach to physics. I had always wanted to know why equations worked, but I was beginning to acquire enough mathematics to actually answer that question occasionally, which was much more satisfying.

Eventually the pandemic brought me back to Nova Scotia. I took a year away from university and, partly because I wanted to experience something resembling a normal life for a while, started bartending in Halifax.

I expected bartending to be a way to make money.

Instead, I ended up doing it for about five years.

I grew genuinely fond of the work and eventually became a senior bartender, including for several years while I was back at Saint Mary's finishing my degree. It gave me a part of my life that had essentially nothing to do with physics, which I think was probably good for me. I made some of my closest friends there, met an enormous number of strange and interesting people, and learned that a restaurant can continue functioning despite apparently violating several laws of thermodynamics.

There is also nothing quite like the friendship produced by surviving a New Year's Eve dinner service together.

When I returned to Saint Mary's, physics gradually started consuming an unreasonable amount of my attention again.

Under Dr. Butler's supervision, I became interested in computational physics and eventually completed my honours thesis on the Harrow-Hassidim-Lloyd quantum algorithm. Quantum computing appealed to me because it involved quantum mechanics, linear algebra, numerical methods, and computer science all at once. It was also my first real experience with the difference between understanding an algorithm on paper and actually trying to make the thing behave on a computer.

What happened next was less planned. I wasn't someone who had spent my undergraduate degree intending to go into condensed matter or materials physics. In fact, when I began looking toward graduate school, most of the physics that had fascinated me for years was still the fundamental stuff: relativity, quantum mechanics, cosmology, and the mathematical structure underneath physical theories.

Then I started talking with Nik Provatas.

Those conversations introduced me to phase-field crystal modelling. I started reading his book, then Daniel's thesis, and somewhere in that process I became rather thoroughly addicted to the subject. Suddenly I was spending my time thinking about free-energy functionals, correlation functions, crystal structures, defects, solidification, and statistical mechanics. This was not where I had expected to end up, but I found that I really liked the problems.

Phase-field crystal models sit in an interesting middle ground. They retain crystalline structure while allowing us to investigate spatial and temporal scales that are difficult to reach using fully atomistic simulations. There is a lot of physics packed into the attempt to describe something as complicated as a material with a tractable free-energy functional, and the more I read about it, the more questions I seem to acquire.

That is more or less how I ended up at McGill, working with Nik and the rest of ACSR.

In retrospect, I don't think the change in subject was as dramatic as it initially seemed. I may have gone from quantum algorithms and an obsession with fundamental physics to thinking about crystalline materials, but the part that keeps me interested is basically the same. I want to know where the mathematical description came from, why it works, which assumptions are hiding inside it, and what happens when you start pulling those assumptions apart.

Apparently that is enough of a research philosophy for the moment.

In 2026, after finishing my degree and my final summer behind the bar, I left Halifax for Montreal to begin graduate studies at McGill.

It is strange to think that all of this can be traced, at least in my own memory, to sitting in a high-school classroom at sixteen and being mildly annoyed that nobody had told me where the kinematic equations came from.

I suppose we'll see where the rabbit hole goes next.

This website is partly a record of that.
